VERBATIM — FEATURE IDEAS (brainstorm notes)
============================================

Two brainstorms, kept here to continue on another machine.
Status: part 1, section 1 (reading comfort) is built on branch
"reading-settings" (the "Aa" menu). Everything else is ideas only.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART 1. BASIC E-BOOK READER FEATURES
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Here are the basic reader features I'd add, roughly in priority order.
Each one notes how it fits with what's already built.

1. Reading comfort (biggest gain, low effort)   [BUILT: "Aa" menu]
- Font size − / +. epubjs can change this for every chapter at once with
  one setting, which is the easy part. One catch: we now lay out the whole
  book up front, so each size change re-flows every chapter. On Karamazov
  (200+ chapters) it may pause for a moment. Highlights and underlines are
  unaffected, because they're tied to paragraphs and character positions,
  not screen positions.
- Font choice: serif, sans-serif or the book's own font. This is the same
  mechanism as font size.
- Line spacing and margins: a small "Aa" menu holding all four settings is
  the usual pattern.
- Theme: light, sepia, dark. epubjs supports this directly. The highlight
  colours would need checking in dark mode: the red and blue underlines are
  fine, but the pale yellow "picked" fill would need a darker variant.
- Remember settings in the browser, so the reader opens the way you left it.

2. Not losing your place
- Resume where you left off for each book. epubjs gives a location marker
  for the current position. In parallel view, saving the left pane's
  position is enough, since the right pane can follow through the alignment.
- Progress: a "Chapter 12 of 95 · 34%" line or a thin progress bar.
- Table of contents: a side drawer that lists the chapters and jumps to
  one. epubjs reads the book's own contents list. In parallel view, jumping
  one pane could also jump the other to the matching chapter, since we
  already know which chapters pair up.

3. Moving around
- Search in the book: find a word and step through the matches. It can
  reuse the code that already reads the book text for alignment.
- Keyboard shortcuts: Page Up/Down, Home/End, and a key for parallel view.
- Synchronised scrolling (a toggle) is the parallel-reader version of "turn
  page". Scroll one side and the other follows to the matching sentence.
  All the pieces exist already (the alignment and the "scroll to
  counterpart" code); it would just run continuously instead of only on
  selection. I think this is the single most valuable addition for this app.

4. Notes and saving
- Bookmarks.
- Your own highlights and notes: a fourth highlight colour alongside
  picked, match and the underlines, saved per book.
- Saved ratings: keep "Rate this translation" results with the sentence,
  so you can build a list of problem passages.

5. Library
- Book cards on the main menu with cover, title, both languages and a
  progress bar, instead of plain links. epubjs can read cover images and
  title data.
- Remove an imported book. Right now imports only add.
- Imports that survive a refresh: imported pairs currently live only in
  that browser tab.

What I'd do first: font size, theme and line spacing in one "Aa" menu,
plus resume-where-you-left-off. These are small, self-contained, and
they're what people notice missing straight away. After that, synchronised
scrolling, because no ordinary reader has it and it builds on what
Verbatim already does.

Two questions that shape the design:
1. Linked or separate settings? Should font size apply to both panes
   together, or to each pane separately? Linked is simpler and keeps the
   sides lined up. Separate helps if one language uses a script (e.g.
   Greek) that reads better larger.
   (Built as: linked, both panes together.)
2. Device or account? Should settings and reading position be saved just
   on this device (browser storage, no setup), or do you want them to
   follow you across devices later (needs a small server-side store)?
   (Built as: this device, browser storage.)

PART 2. JUDGING A TRANSLATION WITH ONLY ONE BOOK
(e.g. just the English translation, no original)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

The honest starting point: without the original, nothing can measure
faithfulness directly. "How well was this translated?" is always a
comparison against something. So the real question is what you compare
against when you only hold the English. I see six angles, from most
reliable to most speculative.

1. Find the original yourself, so one book becomes two.
Most of the books you'd ask this about are classics, and their originals
are usually free: Gutenberg, Wikisource and Perseus (for Greek and Latin)
between them cover a lot. The epub already carries the title, author and
often the translator's name. Verbatim could look up the original-language
edition and download it. From there, everything you've already built
applies: alignment, highlighting, ChatGPT scoring. This gives a real
answer, not a guess, which is why I'd do it first. It mostly fails for
modern books whose original is still under copyright.

2. Compare with other translations of the same book.
This one is quietly powerful. Line up two or three English translations of
the same book against each other. Where every translator says roughly the
same thing, the meaning is almost certainly the author's. Where they split
apart, you've found the spots where the original was hard: wordplay,
ambiguity, cultural references, tone. Those are exactly the spots where
the translator's own reading has entered. You don't need to read the
original language for this. Aligning English with English is also easier
than English with Greek, because the lengths match closely. Classics often
have a free older translation (Garnett, Jowett) alongside the modern ones
you'd own.

3. Ask a model what the original language does here, not what the
original text says.
A model can't reliably recall the exact Russian sentence; if pushed, it
will make one up. But it knows a lot about how Russian, Greek or French
differ from English. So you ask it to explain where English is forced to
lose or choose, for example:
- Russian "ty" and "vy", which mark intimacy or insult and vanish in
  English "you";
- Greek "eros", "philia" and "agape", which all become "love";
- verb aspect;
- grammatical gender;
- names that carry meaning (Karamazov contains "kara", "black";
  Raskolnikov contains "raskol", "schism");
- puns that can't survive.
This is grounded in how the languages work rather than in remembered
text, so it stays reliable. In the reader it would look like a note on a
passage: "Here the original probably used X; English can't keep Y."

4. Read the translation for signs of the translator's own hand.
Translation leaves marks in the English itself, and you can look for them
with only one book:
- Additions: short explanations a native reader wouldn't need ("the kvass,
  a fermented drink, …"). These are usually the translator talking, not
  the author.
- Softening: rough, repetitive or odd original style smoothed into
  polished English. Repetitions disappear and slang becomes neutral.
- Domesticating: foreign idioms swapped for English ones ("raining cats
  and dogs" in a Russian novel is certainly not Dostoevsky).
- Words left untranslated or in italics: the translator signalling
  something they couldn't carry over.
- Footnotes and brackets: explicit evidence of the translator stepping in.
  Ironically, we now strip these out for alignment, but for this feature
  they're gold.
A cheap model can flag these sentence by sentence, the same way as the
current scan, and say why. Note that this measures how much the
translator shows, not whether they're accurate.

5. The translator's voice across their books.
This is the more research-flavoured idea. Constance Garnett translated
Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Chekhov and Turgenev, and readers famously say they
all sound alike. With several books by one translator, you can measure the
style features that stay the same whatever the author: sentence rhythm,
favourite words, punctuation habits. Those features are the translator's
voice. Then in any one book you can mark passages that are typically
"Garnett" rather than typically "Dostoevsky". It needs more books, but it
answers the question "how much of this is not the author's take" more
directly than anything else here.

6. Use the introduction we just removed.
Translators' prefaces often explain their hardest choices ("I have
rendered 'toska' as 'anguish', though…"). A model could read the
introduction, pull out every mention of a specific word or passage, and
attach it as a note where that word appears in the text. It's the
translator's own account of their choices, so it's honest evidence.

How I'd put it together. Each note would say which kind of evidence it
rests on, so you always know how much to trust it:
- Checked against the original: angle 1 found it, so it's as reliable as
  the current alignment.
- Translators disagree here: angle 2. It's factual, but it shows where
  interpretation happened, not which reading is right.
- The language forces a loss here: angle 3. It's general knowledge, not a
  claim about this exact sentence.
- Sounds like the translator's addition: angles 4 and 6. It's a
  judgement, so it should be worded as one.

Cost works the same as today. A cheap whole-book pass flags candidates,
and the expensive explanation runs only when you click a sentence.

Questions still open:
1. Which books? Mostly classics, where angle 1 is easy, or modern novels,
   where you'd rely on angles 2–4?
2. Which question? Do you want "was this sentence accurate?", which needs
   the original, or "what am I missing here?", which angles 3, 4 and 6 can
   answer on their own? They lead to quite different features.
